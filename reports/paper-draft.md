## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Motivation

Japan's cumulative installed solar photovoltaic (PV) capacity grew substantially over the past decade following the government's introduction of a feed-in-tariff (FIT) scheme on July 1, 2012, under the Act on Special Measures Concerning the Procurement of Renewable Energy by Operators of Electric Utilities. The scheme was enacted in the wake of the Fukushima nuclear accident, with an initial tariff of 40 JPY/kWh for installations above 10 kW guaranteed for 10–20 years (Wen, Gao, Kuroki, Gu, & Ren, 2021). By the end of 2022, cumulative capacity had reached 84.9 GW — third-largest among individual countries worldwide, behind China (414.5 GW) and the United States (141.6 GW), and ahead of India (79.1 GW) and Germany (67.2 GW) (IEA PVPS, 2023). That ranking, however, describes an accumulated base rather than current momentum: annual installations in 2022 were 6.5 GW, unchanged from 2021, and the same report characterizes Japan as "a principal market [that] maintains a steady rhythm of new projects but with no market acceleration as elsewhere" — a plateau, not the rapid expansion the market narrative around Japanese PV sometimes assumes. That accumulated, geographically embedded capacity — not the pace of new additions — is what makes the land-use question below immediate rather than prospective. That growth has not been land-use-neutral. Shimada and Takeuchi (2025) show that megawatt-scale, utility-grade PV facilities in Japan are disproportionately sited on forested land, contributing to measurable forest fragmentation at facility edges, while smaller, sub-megawatt installations increasingly occupy agricultural plots — a nontrivial fraction of them already abandoned. Su, Okahashi, and Chen (2018) independently document that farmland abandonment in Japan is itself spatially clustered, concentrated in the Kanto, Chubu, and Chugoku-Shikoku regions, and driven by measurable socioeconomic factors such as farm-household succession rates and land slope. Read together, these two literatures describe a country where renewable-energy expansion and land-use change are entangled in ways that matter for environmental policy, forest conservation, and agricultural land management — but only if the underlying PV inventory used to study that entanglement is itself accurate, current, and complete.

Field surveys and utility interconnection filings cannot supply that inventory at the resolution or update frequency the question requires: they are costly, slow, and — critically for distributed, sub-utility-scale installations — frequently incomplete (Malof, Bradbury, Collins, & Newell, 2016). Satellite-based automated detection is, at present, the only realistic route to a national-scale, regularly updatable PV map. The question this thesis engages with is not *whether* to build such a map from remote sensing — that premise is already well established in the literature reviewed in Chapter 2 — but *which class of detection method* should be used to build it, and specifically whether the answer depends on which installations are being detected.

### 1.2 The methodological tension

Two broad families of detection method compete for this task, and the literature reviewed in Chapter 2 shows neither has been established as a categorical winner. **Spectral, pixel-wise classifiers** — Random Forest, XGBoost, or hand-derived spectral indices — treat each pixel largely independently, deriving a PV/non-PV decision from its spectral reflectance signature (and sometimes hand-engineered texture or topographic features) without reference to the surrounding spatial pattern. These methods have powered several of the largest national-scale PV maps to date, including Zhang, Xu, Wang, Huang, and Xie's (2022) Random Forest map of China and Feng et al.'s (2024) 10-meter-resolution successor, and — most directly relevant here — Shimada and Takeuchi's (2025) XGBoost-plus-spectral-index map of Japan, currently the only published national-scale PV inventory for the country. Their appeal is data efficiency: they can be trained on comparatively small, cheaply produced labeled samples and run at low computational cost across large territories.

**CNN-based approaches**, by contrast, learn spatial and textural patterns directly from image patches — panel arrangement, edge geometry, the visual context of surrounding rooftops or fields — rather than relying on spectral reflectance alone. This is the family of methods behind DeepSolar's near-complete U.S. solar deployment database (Yu, Wang, Majumdar, & Rajagopal, 2018), Kruitwagen et al.'s (2021) global inventory of 68,661 verified PV facilities, and da Costa et al.'s (2021) semantic-segmentation benchmark for Brazil. Their appeal is sensitivity to spatial structure that a per-pixel spectral signature cannot see — but that sensitivity is typically bought with substantially larger annotated training sets and nontrivial GPU compute, a cost spectral methods do not incur.

What makes this tension more than a textbook methods comparison is that it has already played out, once, inside a single research group working on a single problem. Shimada and Takeuchi used a CNN for an earlier, comparable national PV-mapping task in Vietnam (Shimada & Takeuchi, 2023), then moved to a non-CNN, spectral-index-based method for their subsequent Japan study (Shimada & Takeuchi, 2025) — explicitly citing the labeling and compute burden of CNN training as the reason, not any demonstrated accuracy advantage of the spectral approach. That same 2025 paper is unusually candid about where its spectral-only method falls short: it names texture information as an unexplored avenue for improving detection, and documents concrete failure cases a purely spectral classifier structurally cannot resolve — thin-film and other non-crystalline-silicon panels with atypical spectral responses, installations below roughly 50 kW that fall near or below the sensor's effective resolution, and panels whose reflectance signature is blurred by background soil in mixed pixels. Each of these is, in principle, exactly the kind of case where spatial context — panel shape, array geometry, boundary sharpness against the surrounding land cover — could plausibly compensate for an ambiguous spectral signal. Whether it actually does is an open, empirical, and previously untested question.

### 1.3 Research questions

This thesis takes that named limitation, rather than the detection problem in the abstract, as its starting point.

**Primary research question.** Does a CNN-based classifier, which incorporates spatial and textural context rather than pixel-wise spectral values alone, improve solar PV detection accuracy over the spectral-index-and-XGBoost baseline established by Shimada and Takeuchi (2025) — specifically for the three failure cases that study identifies: sub-50 kW installations, non-crystalline-silicon panel materials, and spectrally ambiguous panel arrangements?

**Secondary research question.** How does the label efficiency of the CNN approach compare to the spectral baseline, given that CNN-based classification typically requires substantially more annotated training data than point-based spectral classifiers?

### 1.4 Contribution

This thesis does not propose a new detection architecture. Its contribution is a controlled, same-country, same-data-source comparison targeted at named, specific failure modes rather than a general accuracy comparison drawn across studies that differ in imagery source, geography, and training-set size — the kind of comparison Chapter 2 shows is systematically missing from the existing literature. The result, in either direction, is directly actionable: it informs a concrete design choice for anyone building the next iteration of a national-scale PV monitoring pipeline for Japan, namely whether the added labeling and compute cost of CNN-based detection is justified by measurable gains on the cases that matter, or whether the spectral approach's simplicity is not, in practice, costing much accuracy where it counts.

### 1.5 Structure of this thesis

Chapter 2 reviews the spectral and spatial-context PV-detection literatures separately, situates Japan's own detection lineage within them, and states precisely what gap the present study fills. Chapter 3 describes the imagery, label, and validation data used, including the open questions about label granularity that must be resolved before the experimental pipeline can be finalized. Chapter 4 specifies the three-stage methodology — spectral baseline replication, CNN training, and stratified evaluation — used to answer both research questions. Chapters 5 through 8 (Results, Discussion, Limitations, Conclusion) report and interpret the experimental findings once available.

## 2. Related Work

### 2.1 Spectral and pixel-wise PV mapping

The dominant approach to national- and regional-scale PV mapping to date has been pixel-wise classification on spectral (and, increasingly, texture and topographic) features. Zhang et al. (2022) trained a pixel-based Random Forest classifier on 30-meter Landsat composite imagery within Google Earth Engine to produce the first country-scale PV map of China, subsequently refined with visual interpretation; they report 2,917 km² of mapped PV area and find installations concentrated on cropland, followed by barren land and grassland — an early empirical link between PV siting and land-use class that recurs throughout this literature. Feng et al. (2024) extended this line of work to 10-meter resolution for China's ground-mounted stations specifically, adding Gray-Level Co-occurrence Matrix (GLCM) texture features and topographic covariates (slope, aspect) to a Random Forest classifier trained with active learning, reporting over 89% accuracy — notably, a spectral-family method already reaching for texture features, suggesting the spectral/spatial boundary is porous even within the "spectral" camp.

Most directly relevant to this thesis, Shimada and Takeuchi (2025) produced the only existing national-scale PV map of Japan, using Sentinel-2 imagery from autumn 2022, a purpose-built solar-PV spectral index (PVSI), and an XGBoost classifier, with post-processing and a SHAP-based explainable-AI analysis of siting drivers. They report 571 km² of mapped PV area, and — evaluated against an independent 1,500-point verification sample — an overall accuracy of 0.984, precision of 0.999, recall of 0.969, F1 of 0.984, Cohen's Kappa of 0.968, and a test ROC-AUC of 0.9497 (see §4.1 for the full replication specification). These figures are the headline numbers this thesis's baseline-replication stage will attempt to reproduce independently before drawing any comparative conclusion from them. Their spectral index approach sits alongside He, Tian, and Tian's (n.d.) Solar Photovoltaic Panel Index (SPPI), a physics-based index built from the distinctive PV reflectance peak in the 400–500 nm range, evaluated across five environment types (water, farmland, desert, mountain, urban) and reporting up to 96% overall accuracy in its best-case environment. The SPPI paper is explicit that its motivation is operational efficiency: the authors report their index runs roughly three orders of magnitude faster than deep-learning alternatives, and argue this efficiency, not marginal accuracy loss, is the deciding factor for large-scale operational mapping. Taken as a pair, Shimada and Takeuchi (2025) and He et al. together represent the strongest current articulation of the position this thesis tests empirically: that spectral information alone, well engineered, is sufficient for large-scale PV mapping, and that the added cost of spatial-context modeling is not clearly repaid in accuracy.

### 2.2 Spatial-context CNN PV mapping

The competing family of methods treats PV detection as an image-recognition problem rather than a per-pixel classification problem. Kruitwagen et al. (2021) represent the current high-water mark of this approach at global scale: combining a CNN for spatial detection with an RNN component for estimating installation dates from a longitudinal, multi-year, multi-sensor remote-sensing corpus, they located and verified 68,661 commercial-, industrial-, and utility-scale (>10 kW) PV facilities worldwide — a 432% increase over the previously best available asset-level inventory — and estimated global installed capacity at 423 GW at the end of 2018. Yu et al.'s (2018) DeepSolar preceded this at national (U.S.) scale, using a CNN classifier (with fully connected layers converted to convolutional layers for localization) on very-high-resolution aerial imagery to build a near-complete U.S. solar deployment database, reporting precision in the 93–94% range and recall in the 88–91% range across residential and non-residential installations, and subsequently using the resulting database to identify socioeconomic correlates of solar deployment. Da Costa et al. (2021) took a narrower, more method-focused approach for Brazil, directly benchmarking four semantic-segmentation architectures (U-Net, DeepLabv3+, PSPNet, and Feature Pyramid Network) against four backbone networks (EfficientNet-b0/b7, ResNet-50/101), finding U-Net with an EfficientNet-b7 backbone performed best (98% accuracy, 91% IoU, 95% F-score) but — more importantly for this thesis's methodological design — finding that the four architecture/backbone combinations converged in performance once label quality was controlled for, i.e., that training-label quality mattered more than architecture choice.

Japan has its own, distinct CNN lineage that predates the current spectral-only state of the art. Ishii et al. (2016) trained a CNN from scratch to detect solar power plants in 7-channel multi-spectral Landsat-8 imagery of Japan, converting the network's fully connected layers to convolutional layers to enable detection at arbitrary image resolution, and reporting a significant improvement over the state of the art at the time. Their paper is notable for an explicit ablation over which of the seven available spectral channels to feed the network, finding that combining all channels — not just an RGB subset — was critical to performance; this is, in effect, an early demonstration that spectral richness and spatial-context modeling are not fully independent design axes, a point this thesis returns to in Chapter 6. Imamoglu, Kimura, Miyamoto, Fujita, and Nakamura (2017) built directly on that baseline, proposing a weakly supervised feedback-CNN architecture with multi-channel Pulse-Coupled Neural Network fusion for class-activation-map localization, again on Japan-wide Landsat-8 imagery, commissioned by Japan's New Energy and Industrial Technology Development Organization (NEDO). Neither Ishii et al. (2016) nor Imamoglu et al. (2017) uses architectures from the modern U-Net/DeepLab family of semantic-segmentation networks that dominate the current literature (§2.1's Brazil benchmark being one example), and neither is framed as a controlled comparison against a spectral-index baseline.

### 2.3 Japan-specific precedent and the same-lab pivot

Three papers, taken together, sketch a research trajectory specific to Japan that motivates this thesis's design. Ishii et al. (2016) and Imamoglu et al. (2017) establish that CNN-based detection was demonstrably viable — and outperformed the contemporary state of the art — for Japan as early as 2016–2017. Shimada and Takeuchi's own body of work then illustrates a within-lab pivot away from that approach: their 2023 study used a deep-learning segmentation model on Sentinel-2 imagery to track a multi-year shift in Vietnamese solar PV siting patterns, but their 2025 Japan study — the current state of the art for the country — uses XGBoost and a spectral index instead, citing the labeling and compute demands of CNN training as the reason. Crucially, that stated reason is a resource-constraint argument, not an accuracy claim; the 2025 paper does not report having tested a CNN on the same Japan dataset and found it inferior. Su, Okahashi, and Chen (2018) sit adjacent to this trajectory rather than inside it: their regression analysis of Japanese farmland-abandonment patterns supplies land-use context for *why* PV siting in Japan looks the way Shimada and Takeuchi (2025) describe, but is not itself a detection-methodology paper and is treated in this thesis strictly as background (per the scope decision stated in the exposé), not as a source the detection comparison itself draws on.

### 2.4 Benchmark and comparison-study precedent

A smaller set of papers directly informs this thesis's benchmark design rather than its subject-matter framing. Malof et al. (2016) were among the first to frame PV detection explicitly as a comparison between a Random Forest classifier and a deep CNN on the same aerial-imagery dataset, establishing both the general comparative framing this thesis adopts and one of the first large public U.S. distributed-PV benchmark datasets (135 km² / 2,700 annotated arrays, part of a larger 900 km² / ~20,000-array release). Li et al. (2025) offer a more recent example of a *hybrid* design — a two-stage pipeline using a U-Net to seed positive training samples from sub-meter imagery, followed by a Positive-Unlabeled Random Forest classifier applied at Sentinel-2 scale — that treats the spatial/spectral distinction as a pipeline design choice rather than a binary one, achieving over 90% accuracy in a global, annually updated (2019–2022) PV dataset. Clark and Pacifici (2023) contribute a very-high-resolution (31 cm / 15.5 cm) annotated benchmark dataset intended explicitly to support cross-region generalization testing of detection models, illustrating the broader field's move toward standardized benchmark resources. Droguett and Sanchez's (2024) unpublished Stanford course-project comparison of Mask R-CNN against several YOLO versions is included here only as an illustration of object-detection benchmarking practice on PV imagery — it is not peer-reviewed and is not treated as an authoritative accuracy source anywhere in this thesis.

### 2.5 Research gap

Across the literature reviewed in §2.1–2.4, no study performs a controlled, same-country, same-data-source comparison of a spatial-context CNN against a spectral-signature baseline targeted at named, specific failure modes. Existing spatial-vs-spectral comparisons (Malof et al., 2016; the architecture benchmark internal to da Costa et al., 2021) compare *within* one method family or across generic aggregate accuracy, not against the specific cases a state-of-the-art spectral method already documents as its weak points. The Japan-specific literature in particular shows CNN-based detection was demonstrated to work — and to outperform prior methods — as early as 2016 (Ishii et al.), yet the current national state of the art (Shimada & Takeuchi, 2025) uses a non-CNN method for reasons of cost rather than demonstrated accuracy, and no study has re-opened the CNN question for Japan using a modern segmentation architecture benchmarked directly against that current spectral state of the art. Ishii et al.'s (2016) finding that spectral-channel choice materially affects CNN accuracy further suggests that "spatial" and "spectral" information are not cleanly separable axes in practice — a question this thesis's own channel-selection design (§4.2) will need to engage with directly, and one no existing Japan study has revisited with contemporary architectures.

This thesis is designed to fill exactly that gap: a same-data, same-country, failure-case-targeted comparison of a CNN against Shimada and Takeuchi's (2025) published spectral-index-and-XGBoost baseline, answering the two research questions stated in §1.3. ## 3. Data

### 3.1 Imagery

The primary imagery source is Sentinel-2 Level-2A surface reflectance data, accessed via Google Earth Engine (GEE), restricted to the autumn 2022 acquisition window used by Shimada and Takeuchi (2025). Matching this window exactly — rather than using a more recent or differently dated Sentinel-2 composite — is a deliberate design choice: any comparison between the spectral baseline and the CNN classifier is only informative about the research questions in §1.3 if both models are evaluated on data from the same acquisition period as the published baseline, isolating the detection-method comparison from confounds introduced by seasonal reflectance differences or PV deployment growth between acquisition dates.

### 3.2 Labels

The PV label source is the polygon dataset produced by Shimada and Takeuchi (2025), openly available via Zenodo (DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.10674681). Using the original study's own labels, rather than producing an independent label set, is what makes a same-data comparison possible in the first place; it also means any label noise present in the original dataset is shared identically between the spectral-baseline replication and the CNN classifier (a point returned to as a limitation in Chapter 7, not treated here as a threat to the comparison itself, since it affects both models equally).

**Resolved.** The Zenodo release contains only the final, post-processed PV polygon dataset (571 km² across Japan) — the raw point-level training sample (~60,000 points) and the independent verification sample (1,500 points) that Shimada and Takeuchi used to train and evaluate their XGBoost classifier are not part of the public release. A comparable point sample must therefore be drawn manually from the polygon boundaries and buffer zones, checked against high-resolution imagery, rather than reused directly from the original study.

Reconstructing a comparable sample is nonetheless well specified, because the original sampling protocol is documented in full:

- **Training sample (~60,000 points).** The original study defined 1,000 sampling squares of 1 km × 1 km across Japan: 600 centered within a manually delineated 2020 PV dataset, 300 within a 7 km buffer around populated areas, and 100 manually selected in urban areas specifically to capture non-PV spectral variation. Any unannotated PV installations found inside these squares during labeling were added manually. Random point sampling within the squares yielded 20,000 PV points and 40,000 non-PV points; after removing 88 points with missing Sentinel-2 values, 19,969 PV and 39,943 non-PV points remained, split 80/20 for hyperparameter tuning and training.
- **Independent verification sample (1,500 points).** A separate set — 750 points randomly distributed across the study area, 750 manually placed within known PV sites, yielding a final 752 PV / 748 non-PV split after ground-truthing — was held out entirely from training and used only to report the published accuracy figures (§4.1).

This thesis's own resampling from polygon boundaries should mirror this stratified structure (populated-area buffers, deliberate urban non-PV sampling, an independent held-out verification set never seen during either baseline replication or CNN training) as closely as the polygon-only starting point allows, since deviating from it would confound the baseline-replication validity check in §4.1 with a change in sampling methodology rather than a change in classifier.

### 3.3 Patch extraction for CNN input

The CNN classifier requires fixed-size image patches centered on PV and non-PV locations, rather than the single-pixel feature vectors the spectral baseline uses. Patch centers can now be drawn from the same resampled point set described in §3.2, keeping the two classifiers' training and evaluation locations aligned; patch size, resolution, and any additional negative-sampling strategy beyond the original point protocol remain to be specified during implementation. A spatial (not purely random) train/test split is required regardless, to prevent adjacent, spatially correlated patches from leaking between training and evaluation sets — a risk with any patch-based scheme applied to spatially clustered installations, and a design detail returned to explicitly in Chapter 7 as a mitigated-but-not-eliminated limitation. The original study's independent 1,500-point verification set, once reconstructed, should be held out from CNN training in exactly the same way it was held out from XGBoost training, so that §4.4's stratified evaluation compares both models on genuinely unseen data.

### 3.4 Secondary validation

Government FIT-registered PV capacity statistics, published by Japan's Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry (METI), provide an independent secondary validation source at the prefecture level, consistent with the validation approach used in the original study. This source cannot substitute for pixel- or patch-level ground truth — it reports aggregate installed capacity, not individual installation geometry — but it offers a sanity check on whether the aggregate PV area/capacity estimated by each detection method is directionally consistent with officially registered capacity at a coarser spatial scale.

### 3.5 Failure-case stratification

The primary research question is only answerable if the three failure modes Shimada and Takeuchi (2025) name — sub-50 kW installations, non-crystalline-silicon (e.g., thin-film) panel materials, and spectrally ambiguous panel arrangements — can be identified as discrete, labeled subsets within the evaluation data, separate from the full test set used for aggregate accuracy reporting. Operational definitions for each subset must be established from whatever attribute information the label source (§3.2) provides: installed-capacity or facility-size fields for the sub-50 kW subset, and, more challengingly, panel-material and spectral-ambiguity flags that Shimada and Takeuchi's public release may or may not carry directly, in which case a proxy identification procedure (e.g., manual annotation against Google Earth imagery for a stratified subsample) would be required. This stratification, once defined, is what distinguishes the evaluation protocol in Chapter 4 from a generic aggregate-accuracy comparison: it is the mechanism by which Chapter 5 can report whether any CNN improvement concentrates in exactly the cases the spectral baseline struggles with, or is diffuse across the test set — the specific test the primary research question requires (see also §6.1 in the outline).

## 4. Methodology

The study proceeds in three stages: replication of the spectral baseline, training of the CNN classifier, and stratified evaluation of both.

### 4.1 Spectral baseline replication

The spectral baseline is replicated following Shimada and Takeuchi's (2025) fully specified feature set, pre-masking rules, classifier, and post-processing pipeline.

**Input features (9 total), from Sentinel-2 Level-2A surface reflectance.** Six reflectance bands are used directly — Blue (B2, 490 nm), Green (B3, 560 nm), Red (B4, 665 nm), NIR (B8, 842 nm), SWIR1 (B11, 1610 nm), and SWIR2 (B12, 2190 nm) — together with three derived spectral indices fed to the classifier as additional features:

$$\text{NDVI} = \frac{\text{B8} - \text{B4}}{\text{B8} + \text{B4}} \qquad \text{MNDWI} = \frac{\text{B3} - \text{B11}}{\text{B3} + \text{B11}} \qquad \text{PVSI} = \left(\frac{2\,\text{B11} - \text{B12} - \text{B8}}{2\,\text{B11} + \text{B12} + \text{B8}}\right) \times \frac{\text{B2}}{\text{B4}}$$

Note a discrepancy worth stating explicitly, since the exposé (§5) characterizes the feature set as "reflectance bands together with NDVI, MNDWI, PVSI, and BSI": in the original study, BSI (Bare Soil Index, $\text{BSI} = \frac{\text{B2}-\text{B4}}{\text{B2}+\text{B4}}$) is **not** one of the nine features passed to XGBoost. It is instead used in a separate rule-based pre-masking step, alongside NDVI and MNDWI, to filter out obvious non-PV land cover before classification: pixels with NDVI > 0.5 (dense vegetation), MNDWI > 0 (water bodies), or BSI > 0.2 (bright blue synthetic roofing/plastics, which would otherwise be confused with PV materials) are excluded from candidate consideration. This thesis's replication follows the original protocol — masking, not a ninth feature — rather than the exposé's shorthand description, since the goal is a faithful reproduction of the published baseline.

**Classifier.** An XGBoost model, with hyperparameters tuned via Optuna (100 iterations, 5-fold cross-validation): `max_depth = 8`, `eta = 0.01717`, `n_estimators = 1000`. The model outputs a per-pixel PV-class probability ($P(\text{PV})$) rather than a hard label directly. PVSI is reported as the single most influential feature by Gini gain — worth flagging as a candidate discussion point in Chapter 6, since it means the baseline's accuracy rests heavily on one hand-engineered index, which is itself a testable hypothesis about *why* a CNN might or might not improve on it (a CNN that cannot recover PVSI's discriminative power from raw bands would be a informative negative result).

**Post-processing.** The raw probability raster is denoised with a white top-hat morphological filter (1-pixel-radius disk), thresholded at median $P(\text{PV}) \geq 0.6$, segmented into coherent facility polygons using Felzenszwalb graph-based segmentation, and filtered to remove small polygons falling within Densely Inhabited Districts (DIDs), which eliminates a class of urban false positives. This post-processing pipeline is replicated identically for the baseline; whether an equivalent post-processing step is applied to the CNN's raw output (§4.2) — and if so, whether it should be the same pipeline or a CNN-appropriate alternative — is a design decision this thesis must make explicit rather than leave implicit, since post-processing choices can materially affect the polygon-level accuracy figures both models are ultimately compared on.

**Published reference benchmark**, evaluated on the independent 1,500-point verification sample (§3.2): overall accuracy 0.984, precision 0.999, recall 0.969, F1 0.984, Cohen's Kappa 0.968, test ROC-AUC 0.9497.

Replicating the baseline independently, rather than relying solely on these published figures, serves two purposes: it is a validity check on the replication pipeline itself (§5.1 will report how closely the replicated baseline reproduces them before the figures are used as a comparison point for anything else), and it guarantees that any subsequent CNN-vs-baseline comparison is conducted against a baseline evaluated under conditions identical to the CNN's own evaluation — same resampled points, same evaluation period, same label source, same held-out verification set — rather than against a number taken from a different paper's own held-out set.

### 4.2 CNN classifier

The CNN classifier is trained on image patches centered on the same PV and non-PV locations used for the spectral baseline (§3.3), using either a compact architecture trained from scratch or a pretrained backbone (ResNet or EfficientNet family) fine-tuned on the patch dataset. This decision is deliberately left open pending the patch-dataset size that results from resolving §3.2's open item: da Costa et al.'s (2021) Brazil benchmark finding — that architecture and backbone choice converged in performance once label quality was controlled for — argues against over-investing effort in an extensive architecture search relative to getting the label pipeline right, and is treated here as the methodological justification for that prioritization. Input channel selection (how many, and which, Sentinel-2 bands the CNN receives) must be decided with reference to Ishii et al.'s (2016) finding that combining all available spectral channels, not an RGB subset, was critical to their CNN's performance on the same country's imagery; this thesis's own channel choice is revisited explicitly in the discussion of Chapter 6 as a direct extension of that 2016 finding to a modern architecture.

### 4.3 Optional extension: frozen-embedding foundation-model comparison

Time permitting, a frozen-embedding comparison against a self-supervised remote-sensing foundation model may be added as a lower-cost extension: rather than fine-tuning a full backbone, this would evaluate whether embeddings from a pretrained foundation model, combined with a lightweight classifier head, reach comparable performance to the fully fine-tuned CNN at a fraction of the training cost — directly relevant to the secondary research question's concern with label and compute efficiency. This extension is explicitly a stretch goal, not core to answering either research question, and is scoped accordingly in the project timeline (§8 of the exposé; reproduced in `reports/paper-outline.md`).

### 4.4 Evaluation protocol

Both the replicated spectral baseline and the CNN classifier are evaluated on the full test set using the same metrics as the original study — overall accuracy, precision, recall, F1, Cohen's Kappa, and ROC-AUC — to preserve direct comparability with the published baseline figures (§4.1). Both models are then evaluated separately on the stratified failure-case subsets defined in §3.5, using the same metric set. This second evaluation is the one that actually answers the primary research question: whether any aggregate CNN improvement over the baseline is concentrated in the sub-50 kW, non-crystalline-silicon, or spectrally-ambiguous subsets specifically, or is spread diffusely across the test set in a way that would suggest the CNN is not, in fact, addressing the failure modes the spectral baseline's own authors identified.

### 4.5 Label-efficiency comparison

To address the secondary research question, both models are additionally trained on a series of shrinking fractions of the available training data, with accuracy and F1 recorded at each fraction to produce learning curves for direct comparison. A CNN that requires substantially more labeled data than the spectral baseline to reach comparable accuracy would confirm the labeling-cost concern Shimada and Takeuchi (2025) cite as their reason for choosing the non-CNN approach; a CNN that reaches comparable accuracy at similar label volumes would undercut that reasoning for future Japan-scale PV mapping efforts.

### 4.6 Compute and reproducibility

Model training runs locally on an RTX 2070 Super, with Kaggle's free GPU tier available as a fallback if patch volumes exceed what is practical to train locally. This constraint is stated here as a methodological fact, not merely an implementation detail: any conclusions about label or compute efficiency (§4.5) are conditioned on training being feasible within this hardware envelope, and any architecture or patch-volume decisions made to fit within it are reported explicitly in Chapter 5 as part of the experimental record, since they bear directly on how the secondary research question's answer should be interpreted and generalized.
